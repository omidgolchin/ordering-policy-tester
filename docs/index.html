The smoothing trade-off calmer orders or lower cost

Order-up-to with smoothing closes only a fraction of the gap to your target each week. A smaller fraction calms your orders (a lower bullwhip ratio) but reacts slowly when demand moves, which can cost more. Disney and Towill (2003) map this trade-off; here it is for your settings, at your stage.

How the verdict is decided simulation, formulas, sources

The chain is simulated week by week for 36 weeks. It opens in steady state: pipelines full at the opening demand level (the median of the first four weeks) and every stage holding its safety stock, so demand that never moves leaves every rule at zero cost (tested). Each week a stage receives goods, gets its incoming order, ships what it can, pays holding cost on what is left and shortage cost on what it owes, then places its order. Deterministic demand patterns are simulated once; random demand is simulated over 20 demand paths and the verdict uses the median, with the middle 80% range shown.

Order-up-to target. S = (forecast weekly demand × lead time) + z × (demand spread × √lead time), where z comes from the service level you pick (1.64 for 95%): the standard base-stock target (Silver, Pyke and Thomas, 2016). The lead time is order delay plus shipping delay (the factory, which produces, has only the shipping delay). The safety stock is set once from the spread of demand; the forecast is the average of the last few weeks you choose, of the orders the stage receives or, with information sharing, of customer demand. Forecasting from orders is exactly how the bullwhip starts (Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang, 1997). "Balanced to my costs" sets the service level to the critical ratio, shortage ÷ (shortage + holding): the point where one more unit of target saves as much shortage cost as it adds in holding cost.

Bullwhip ratio. The variance of a stage's orders divided by the variance of customer demand (Chen, Drezner, Ryan and Simchi-Levi, 2000). 1.0 means orders are exactly as steady as demand. Chen and colleagues show it grows with the lead time and shrinks as the forecast window lengthens, which the forecast-window check shows for your settings.

Smoothing. The proportional order-up-to rule orders the forecast plus a fraction of the remaining gap between the target and the inventory position (Disney and Towill, 2003), so with steady demand it orders exactly demand. The tool tries factors from 0.2 to 1.0 and keeps the cheapest at your stage; near ties go to the calmer orders.

The anchoring bot. In Play mode, a teammate can use the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic Sterman (1989) fitted to real players: expected demand updates by 0.36 of the surprise, and the order adds 0.26 of the gap to the target stock, counting only 0.34 of the supply line. It is the pattern real people fall into, and it whipsaws the chain.
